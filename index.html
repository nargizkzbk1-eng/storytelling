Introduction

The Blindness of Habit

What we stop seeing

We stop seeing what surrounds us every day. Caught up in routines, news feeds and internet gossip, we rarely look up from the screen, and so we rarely ask what this city looked like yesterday or what it will become tomorrow.

The city itself has not been so forgetful. It has been renamed five times since 1961.

5
  1. 1961Tselinograd
  2. 1992Akmola
  3. 1998Astana
  4. 2019Nur-Sultan
  5. 2022Astana, again

Names of the city, 1961–2022

The capital moved here in 1997. By May 2026, driverless light-rail trains were already gliding above the left bank. Official news keeps the gloss: glass towers, glowing domes, grand openings.

Street photography works differently. It does not wait for historic milestones. It records what a street looks like on an ordinary evening, and that is why a casual snapshot taken just for fun, without filters or tourist landmarks, can end up as the only honest historical record future generations will have.

ChapterI

What isn’t on Google

Family albums of Tselinograd

A city’s memory does not live in official decrees. It lives in boxes and photo albums on the top shelves at home.

My first interviewee, Laila Nurlybaevna, came to Tselinograd as a child and has lived here for two or three decades. I asked what strangers a hundred years from now would think if only random street photos survived. She smiled at how fast the city grows more beautiful. Then came a regret.

It’s a real pity this isn’t on social media. There are no old photographs of old Tselinograd there, but friends and relatives have them in their albums. Very interesting ones, of the old city.

— Laila Nurlybaevna

The pictures exist. They are just not where the next generation looks first. To see the city before the glass skyscrapers, we opened her personal archive and went through it frame by frame.

The archive of Laila Nurlybaevna01 / 04

For Laila, the old city meant open space: big streetlamps, big shop windows on Respublika Avenue, the K-700 tractor monument at its heart. Today, she says, life boils on both banks, especially the left, where new buildings carry a new chapter of the country’s history.

ChapterII

A city at LRT speed

Vanishing details

If older residents measure the city against Tselinograd, younger locals watch it change at speed. Dilnaz Zhusupova grew up near the Seventh Polyclinic. She loves the aesthetic energy of modern Astana: the new station district, trendy cafés where young bloggers pose, the newly launched LRT.

But when I asked what she misses, she named no landmark.

I probably miss the open-air market most. It’s tied up with childhood. When we were kids we loved going to the market, shopping outdoors, and it had its own kind of vibe.

— Dilnaz Zhusupova

Behind her delight in the city’s new vibe sits a quiet realization: familiar places are vanishing fast. The streets around her childhood school are filling up with residential complexes and shopping malls.

I asked what must be photographed now, before it is gone in twenty years. She gave a clear to-do list for any street photographer.

ChapterIII

Voices on the pavement

Two conversations, as recorded

Both interviews were recorded on the street, in Russian. Press play to hear the originals. The English below is my selection and translation of what was said.

Interview 01

Laila Nurlybaevna

Visited Tselinograd as a child, has lived in the city for two or three decades.

0:00
What would people say in 100 years, with only random phone photos left?
They’ll probably be surprised how fast time flies, and how the city keeps transforming, more and more beautiful.
Which place still feels warm to you?
Where the K-700 tractor stood. It was the symbol of Tselinograd. Then came Respublika Avenue, the epicentre: the most beautiful shops, museums, cinemas, even restaurants, all on that one street.
Where would you take a friend who has never been here?
Life boils on both banks now, especially the left. It’s a new city, but there is something in those buildings that speaks of our country’s history. I’d take them there.

Interview 02

Dilnaz Zhusupova

Grew up near the Seventh Polyclinic and studied in the same district.

0:00
When did you realize the city had changed?
Right now, probably with the LRT, and with the new districts. Nurzhol, the New Railway Station: it has become beautiful, with very aesthetic cafés. Girls, young models, everyone shoots there as if abroad, for blogs.
Three places for a visitor?
First the park along the Ishim river. Then a ride on the LRT to see the beautiful places. And a walk around Khan Shatyr, Mega and the Bayterek boulevard.
What should be photographed right now?
I photographed the old bus stops. They’re changing now, but some are still there. And the theatres on the right bank. I’d keep them as pictures, the way our parents did.
ChapterIV

Through my lens

Cutting the towers to see the streets

Dilnaz’s list became a test for my own camera. Going through everything I shot around Astana, I saw that my lens had gone to the landmarks first.

I set aside the floodlit skyscrapers. They were my best-lit pictures, and they showed what everyone’s phone already holds. I cut them, keeping only Bayterek as a postcard benchmark to measure the rest of the city against.

What remained were ordinary, grey streets, the closest visual record of what residents actually miss.

Street photography does not wait for a headline. It captures an ordinary Tuesday before it turns into history.

Conclusion

An archive for a hundred years ahead

What will be left

A hundred years from now, a stranger scrolling through the surviving gigabytes of our smartphones will see the glow of the left bank, because we loved photographing the light.

But if the casual, unpolished snapshots survive beside it, a wet crossroads, an old bus stop, strangers in winter coats, family photographs from Tselinograd, the future gets a chance to see how the city truly lived. It will remember the people who stayed outside the frame of official news.

Reporting

Contemporary street photographs were shot by the author in Astana. Historical photographs come from Laila Nurlybaevna’s personal family archive. Passers-by were photographed from behind or at a distance to respect privacy.
